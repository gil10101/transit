STANDINGS · CLOSED JUDGED DAYS ONLY

Reliability, city by city

Service-weighted across every closed judged day; routes below a 50% completeness floor are excluded rather than guessed. A composite 0–100 score requires 20 judged days across a city's judged window; New York is the first to clear it, scoring 85.8 on 2026-08-23–09-11. The rest still show provisional evidence rather than a verdict — the bar refuses a score rather than dressing up a thin one.

On-time = arrival within −1 to +5 min of schedule. Excess wait = time beyond the scheduled headway on frequent service. Added (unscheduled) trips count toward volume, never toward on-time.

PIPELINE · WHAT THE TESTS CAUGHT

The warehouse behind the numbers

Pollers fetch each agency's GTFS-RT into Kafka; Spark Structured Streaming lands it in S3 Iceberg; a Dagster chain drains, re-pins Snowflake, and rebuilds the dbt marts twice a day. These are the finds that made the numbers trustworthy:

Zurich was a delay-only feed

97% of Swiss rows carry a stated delay with no timestamps. Finalization demanded timestamps and silently cut the city to ~3k events a day. Fixed by computing actuals as schedule + stated delay — Zurich jumped to ~550k events a day and first place on on-time share.

A regex ate the 7 train

Snowflake string literals consume backslashes, so a direction regex quietly matched the wrong character and every northbound 7-line trip vanished between silver and gold. A completeness floor test went red; the fix ships with a contract test so it cannot come back.

TTC publishes two schedules

Toronto scored 0.8% stop overlap until it turned out the city ships two GTFS files and the pipeline read the one that pairs with nothing. The one that pairs with its realtime matches 99.6% — the coverage gap was ours, not the agency's.

A replay, rolled back exactly

A replacement Spark checkpoint replayed Kafka retention as ~67M duplicate rows. Caught at the 75-minute mark; Iceberg's snapshot rollback reversed it to the byte, and the gap is documented in the incident log rather than papered over.

One 10 MB file, 2,880 times a day

The Swiss alerts endpoint returned a byte-identical 10 MB payload on every 30-second poll — 29 GB a day of one repeated file, most of the raw bucket. Fixed with per-endpoint cadence; the alerts product now polls every 10 minutes.

The schedule that never fired

EventBridge Scheduler's context token was passed through un-substituted, so every trigger failed an API regex — found by reading the dead-letter queue, not the green console. Replaced with a Lambda invoker; the first hands-off drain is in the log.

METHOD · HOW THE SCORING WORKS

Same rules for every city

Realtime predictions are finalized against each agency's own schedule: a stop event is on time within −1 to +5 minutes. Cities publishing no usable delay get it computed from predicted arrival vs schedule; Zurich and Tokyo state delays directly and are labeled as such. Frequent service is judged on excess wait time instead of the timetable — riders there experience headways, not clock times.

The scorecard refuses to rank a city on fewer than 20 closed judged days in a month; routes under 10 scheduled trips or a 50% completeness floor are excluded and counted, never guessed. Known coverage gaps (Helsinki ferries ride an AIS feed we don't poll; one tram line has never emitted realtime) are documented in the repo instead of smoothed over.
