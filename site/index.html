STANDINGS · CLOSED JUDGED DAYS ONLY

Reliability, city by city

Service-weighted across every closed judged day; routes below a 50% completeness floor are excluded rather than guessed. A composite 0–100 score requires 20 judged days across a city's judged window; New York is the first to clear it, scoring 85.8 on 2026-08-23–09-11. The rest still show provisional evidence rather than a verdict — the bar refuses a score rather than dressing up a thin one.

On-time = arrival within −1 to +5 min of schedule. Excess wait = time beyond the scheduled headway on frequent service. Added (unscheduled) trips count toward volume, never toward on-time.

Tokyo's wait and bunching figures are not measured the way the other seven are, and should not be read as a win. Toei publishes a stated delay instead of an arrival time, so an arrival is reconstructed as schedule + stated delay — and because 91% of those stated delays are exactly zero, 90.4% of Tokyo's gaps between consecutive trains are identical to the scheduled gap, against 0.2–4.1% everywhere else. Its excess wait and bunching are therefore close to the timetable reflected back rather than observed irregularity. The same caveat already applies to its on-time share. Cancellations stay blank for a different reason: odpt:Train has no cancellation field at all.

METHOD · HOW THE SCORING WORKS

Same rules for every city

Realtime predictions are finalized against each agency's own schedule: a stop event is on time within −1 to +5 minutes. Cities publishing no usable delay get it computed from predicted arrival vs schedule; Zurich and Tokyo state delays directly and are labeled as such. Frequent service is judged on excess wait time instead of the timetable — riders there experience headways, not clock times.

The scorecard refuses to rank a city on fewer than 20 closed judged days in a month; routes under 10 scheduled trips or a 50% completeness floor are excluded and counted, never guessed. Known coverage gaps (Helsinki ferries ride an AIS feed we don't poll; one tram line has never emitted realtime) are documented in the repo instead of smoothed over.

SPECS · FINE PRINT

What it runs on

Ingest

Eight poller containers, one per city, from a single config-driven image. Thirty endpoints at a 30-second floor — SF at 200s under a 60 requests/hour cap, Zurich's alerts at 600s. Every response is archived to S3 before anything decodes it, on a 7-day expiry.

Queue

Single-broker Kafka in KRaft mode on a t4g.small with 48 GB gp3. Four topics at three partitions each, zstd compression, 50 ms linger, 10 MB maximum message, 24-hour retention.

Processing

Spark Structured Streaming on EMR Serverless 7.5.0 with an availableNow trigger. The application is capped at 8 vCPU / 32 GB; each run is a 2 vCPU / 6 GB driver plus two 2 vCPU / 10 GB executors, 16 shuffle partitions, five state-store versions retained.

Lake

Apache Iceberg on S3 through a Hadoop catalog. Bronze is partitioned by (feed, city, service_date), silver by (city, service_date). Snowflake reads silver as external tables, so there is no load step.

Warehouse

Snowflake on an X-Small warehouse. dbt builds 17 staging views, 19 intermediate tables and 13 marts over 6 seeds, with 244 tests on every run.

Orchestration

Dagster on a t4g.medium services box: drains every two hours, the dbt chain at 11:05 and 23:05 UTC, a feed-freshness tripwire four times an hour, hourly weather, and a schedule refresh on Sunday mornings.

Gross AWS usage was $46.92 for 1–12 September 2026 — EMR Serverless $16.86, S3 $14.60, EC2 $8.96, the remainder networking and registry. Credits cover it, so the invoice is $0.00. Every figure on this page is read from the Snowflake catalog, the AWS API or the orchestrator's own run log rather than estimated.
